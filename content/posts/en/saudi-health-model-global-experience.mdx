> *This post is a translation of the original Arabic article.*

Every health system reflects the history, population, economy, and institutions of its country. Saudi Arabia is selecting mechanisms that have delivered value across global systems and adapting them to the local context.

The idea of regional organizations carrying responsibility for a defined population appears across several systems. Primary care plays a major role in organizing the patient journey in the [**English health system**](https://www.england.nhs.uk/integratedcare/what-is-integrated-care/). Population-based funding and strategic purchasing appear in different forms across [**Canada**](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/canada-health-care-system.html), [**Australia**](https://www.health.gov.au/our-work/phn/what-PHNs-are), and the [**United States**](https://www.cms.gov/priorities-innovation-key-concepts-accountable-care-accountable-care-organizations).

Saudi Arabia combines these mechanisms inside a model built around national priorities.

The state guarantees [**comprehensive coverage**](https://www.who.int/health-topics/universal-health-coverage) for citizens. [**Health clusters**](https://www.health.sa/en/clusters) manage care across defined areas. The funding entity purchases services. The Ministry of Health focuses on regulation and oversight. The [**Saudi Model of Care**](https://www.health.sa/en/model-of-care) gives prevention, primary care, and digital services central roles.

The value comes from selecting each mechanism with purpose.

Funding allocation requires accurate population data. Referral management requires [**strong primary care**](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/primary-health-care). Cluster independence requires financial and operating capabilities with clear governance. [**Purchasing from private providers**](https://www.who.int/activities/promoting-strategic-purchasing) requires contracts and quality indicators. [**Digital services**](https://www.waelkabli.com/en/blog/ai-in-digital-health) require integration with the care journey.

Successful adaptation starts with understanding why a mechanism worked in its original system, followed by building the local conditions that allow it to work in Saudi Arabia.

The Saudi model has an important advantage: the ability to build a large national system with room for development across [**20 health clusters**](https://www.health.sa/en/clusters), each serving a distinct population and set of needs.

This structure supports learning, comparison, and exchange of operating practices. Over time, Saudi Arabia can produce health-system knowledge for other countries pursuing universal coverage, efficiency, and digital transformation within one national model.
