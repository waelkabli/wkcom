> *This post is a translation of the original Arabic article.*

In one consulting engagement, two Saudi interns joined us from the client side. Our scope included teaching them, transferring knowledge, and helping them understand the project. Some consultants treated them as peripheral — limited to a training role — and directed their energy toward the decision-makers of the moment.

My view was different. Our success as consultants meant delivering the project and building capabilities the client could rely on after we left. So I treated them as a meaningful part of the client team. I set clear deliverables I committed to on their behalf. I included them in discussions, explained the reasoning behind decisions, made expectations clear, and listened to their questions the same way I would with any other stakeholder.

Months later, major organizational changes swept through the client. Several senior officials departed. The two interns became decision-makers on the project — with signing authority on project deliverables. That's when the value of involving them from day one became obvious.

The surface read of this story: respect junior people because they might rise someday.

The read I actually believe: respect everyone you work with. The quality of your leadership shows in how you treat all people, regardless of their current title or authority.

## Developing National Talent Is a Core Responsibility

We talk a lot about supporting national talent and transferring knowledge. Saudi Vision 2030's [Human Capability Development Program](https://www.vision2030.gov.sa/en/explore/programs/human-capability-development-program) made this a cornerstone of the national agenda.

But the real test happens inside projects. It starts when we give young professionals a genuine opportunity to understand and participate — when we assign them real responsibilities, open space for experimentation, and let them make decisions. Talent grows through participation. It needs context to understand decisions, clear expectations to guide its work, and a leader willing to invest time in its development.

Developing talent is a responsibility led by managers and leaders, as [Harvard Business Review Arabia](https://hbrarabic.com/%D9%84%D8%A7-%D8%AA%D9%81%D9%88%D8%B6-%D8%A5%D8%AF%D8%A7%D8%B1%D8%A9-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%85%D9%88%D8%A7%D9%87%D8%A8-%D9%84%D9%84%D9%85%D9%88%D8%A7%D8%B1%D8%AF-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A8%D8%B4%D8%B1%D9%8A%D8%A9/) makes clear. A leader's direct presence in the development journey gives it weight and makes it part of the daily work, not a side activity.

In consulting and in projects broadly, our responsibility extends from delivering recommendations to building the capacity to execute them. The classic literature in this field confirms that [consulting is more than giving advice](https://hbr.org/1982/09/consulting-is-more-than-giving-advice).

The good consultant leaves behind a stronger organization, a team more capable of making decisions, and knowledge that outlasts the project.

This is part of a leadership philosophy I've held for years: [servant leadership](https://hbrarabic.com/%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%82%D9%8A%D8%A7%D8%AF%D8%A9-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AE%D8%A7%D8%AF%D9%85%D8%A9-%D9%88%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A7%D8%AD%D8%AA%D8%B1%D8%A7%D9%82-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%88%D8%B8%D9%8A%D9%81%D9%8A/) — measuring the impact of your leadership by how many people became stronger because of their time working with you.

## Stakeholder Management Starts with Understanding People

When we talk about "building relationships" or "stakeholder management," the map usually starts with a set of questions:

* Who holds the decision?
* Who can open the door?
* Who influences the outcome?
* Who holds the knowledge?
* Who facilitates execution?
* And who will own responsibility after delivery?

These questions help us understand the project. The map becomes more valuable when it also captures each party's needs, expectations, and role in the success of the work.

[Stakeholder management starts with understanding their expectations](https://hbrarabic.com/%D9%82%D9%8A%D8%A7%D8%AF%D8%A9-%D8%A3%D8%B5%D8%AD%D8%A7%D8%A8-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%85%D8%B5%D9%84%D8%AD%D8%A9-%D9%84%D9%84%D8%A7%D8%B3%D8%AA%D8%B1%D8%A7%D8%AA%D9%8A%D8%AC%D9%8A%D8%A9/), then clarifying scope, maintaining information flow, and involving the right people at the right time.

A deep professional relationship is built from:

* Quality of engagement at every stage.
* Keeping small promises.
* Transferring knowledge quietly, consistently.
* Managing expectations clearly. (Your motto: no surprises.)
* Disagreeing with respect. (Disagree on the work; stay friends with the person.)
* Ending the engagement with mutual regard intact.

These behaviors compound. They build professional and human capital that lasts for years.

## Authority Is a Temporary State

Titles help us define roles:

* This one is a client.
* That one is a vendor.
* This one is an intern.
* That one is a director.

Then life moves. People transition. Organizations restructure. The intern becomes an executive. The employee becomes a client. The founder becomes an investor, a partner, or a competitor. The person with consistent standards maintains the quality of their engagement regardless. As roles change, their relationships remain clear and trustworthy.

This is where servant leadership shows its value. The leader uses their experience and authority to remove obstacles, clarify direction, and help others succeed. A leader's standing grows from their impact on people — and it outlasts the title.

## Managing Expectations Is a Form of Respect

Strong professional relationships grow from clear expectations:

* A realistic promise.
* A defined delivery date.
* Early notice when circumstances change.
* A clear explanation for any scope change.
* Involving relevant parties before decisions are made.
* No surprises.

This is why [setting and managing expectations](https://hbrarabic.com/%D8%A5%D8%AF%D8%A7%D8%B1%D8%A9-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AA%D9%88%D9%82%D8%B9%D8%A7%D8%AA-%D9%81%D9%8A-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%85%D8%B4%D8%A7%D8%B1%D9%8A%D8%B9/) ranks among the most critical skills in project leadership. Managing expectations means being clear from the start: what will we deliver? What do we need from the other party? What are the risks? When do we reassess?

The news might be hard. Clarity is still the foundation of trust. When your words align with your actions, the other party knows how to work with you and plan around your commitments. Even a refusal becomes professional and respectful when it comes early, stated plainly, with a reason that makes sense.

## Firmness Protects the Relationship

Maintaining relationships includes the ability to make hard decisions and manage performance clearly.

You can:

* Decline a request.
* Hold someone accountable for a result.
* Redefine the scope.
* End a contract.
* Disagree with a decision.
* Exit a partnership.

In every one of these situations, you can still preserve the other person's dignity and separate the problem from the person's value. Firmness protects the relationship when it clarifies responsibilities and expectations. Clarity, consistency, and respect give difficult decisions a professional and fair frame. The other person may leave the meeting stung by the decision — and still recognize that you were fair and clear. That outcome preserves respect and leaves the door open for a mature future relationship.

## Knowledge Moves Through Trust

In organizations, relationships are human value and the network through which knowledge travels.

An employee asks a trusted colleague before escalating a problem formally. An intern raises questions when they feel safe. An organization calls a leader it trusts when facing a new situation. A team asks for help early when it knows the question will be welcomed.

This is why [psychological safety is a foundational element of high-performing teams](https://hbrarabic.com/%D8%A5%D9%86%D8%B4%D8%A7%D8%A1-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A3%D9%85%D8%A7%D9%86-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%86%D9%81%D8%B3%D9%8A/). It gives individuals space to ask questions, admit mistakes, and flag problems while they're still small.

High trust shortens meetings and approvals, accelerates knowledge sharing, and gives teams greater coordination capacity.

Knowledge transfer also deepens when organizations build cultures and systems that [encourage experts to share their expertise and develop their peers](https://hbrarabic.com/%D9%85%D9%86%D8%B9-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AE%D8%A8%D8%B1%D8%A7%D8%A1-%D9%85%D9%86-%D8%A7%D8%AD%D8%AA%D9%83%D8%A7%D8%B1-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%85%D8%B9%D8%B1%D9%81%D8%A9/).

The best organizations combine trust with clear rules. Good relationships support the system. A good system protects the relationships.

## What Remains After the Project?

Every project ends. Every position changes. Even companies merge, close, or redefine themselves.

What stays with a person:

* Their reputation for following through.
* The people they learned from and helped learn.
* The talent they gave a real opportunity.
* How they managed expectations in hard moments.
* The impression they left on people under pressure.
* A network that can say: we disagreed with him, and he was fair.

A ten-year study found that the most distinguished leaders deliver results and [build close relationships grounded in mutual trust](https://hbrarabic.com/%D8%B3%D9%85%D8%A7%D8%AA-%D8%AA%D9%85%D9%8A%D8%B2-%D9%85%D8%B3%D8%A4%D9%88%D9%84-%D8%AA%D9%86%D9%81%D9%8A%D8%B0%D9%8A-%D8%B9%D9%86-%D8%A2%D8%AE%D8%B1/) — and they show genuine interest in the people delivering those results alongside them.

That is the standard of leadership: deliver the result, build the capability, preserve the dignity of people, and leave behind relationships built on trust and respect.

Work hard. Be firm. Manage stakeholders with professionalism. Protect the outcome and manage expectations clearly. Treat people as partners in the journey — people whose impact extends well beyond the project.

The project will end. The title will change.

But the impression left by how you treated people will last far longer than you expect.
